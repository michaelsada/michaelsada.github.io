How I work

Part → Material → Process → Prototype

STEP 1

Part

Define what it has to do: the loads, the fit, and the off-the-shelf hardware it has to work with.

STEP 2

Material

Choose for the job. Rigid PLA for structure, flexible TPU where a part has to grip or cushion.

STEP 3

Process

Pick the process and print settings, and design for them from the first sketch, tolerances included.

STEP 4

Prototype

Print, measure, adjust. Iterate until the fit is right, then document it.

Lead designer · 2024 · Modesto Junior College

Autonomous Solar Tracker

RoleLead designer
Printed parts8 (PLA + TPU)
Hardware1/4-20 & M2 fasteners, bearings
ElectronicsArduino Uno, servo, solar cell
ResultEvery printed part fit on the first print

What it does

An Arduino Uno rotates the panel's mast a full 360° through a servo and timing belt, records the solar cell's output along the way, and returns to the angle that produced the most power. The panel's tilt is set by hand at a pivot and locked in place.

My rules

  • Use specific off-the-shelf fasteners and bearings, with no glue anywhere.
  • Use as few parts as possible.
  • Everything comes fully apart and goes back together.

Design details

  • Locking pivot. The panel tilts on a 1/4-20 bolt with a hex nut captured in the print, so it can't spin loose once tightened.
  • Adjustable belt tension. The servo seat is slotted, so sliding the servo back pulls the timing belt tight against the mast.
  • Glue-free pulley. A conical TPU press-fit bushing grips the pulley to the mast.
  • Bearing housings. Two printed housings, Ø32 × 40 mm with a 22 mm bore, seat the ball bearings the mast spins in.
  • Mounting holes for the Arduino, solar cell, servo and battery pack are designed into the prints.

One evening, eight parts

The first round of parts didn't fit the hardware we had chosen. Instead of patching them, I restarted from scratch one Friday evening and designed all eight printed parts in about eight hours. Because I built FDM tolerances into the CAD from the start, every part fit on the first print.

Client capstone · 2026 · Modesto Junior College

RF Key Finder Enclosure

ClientCustom RF key finder
CADOnshape
PrototypeFDM 3D printing
Production intentInjection-molded ABS
Iterations9+
Lid rim0.5 mm

The brief

A client needed a slim enclosure for their RF key finder's circuit board and battery. It mounts flush to the back of a phone case and hides under a pop socket. My printed prototype let the client test the fit and feel before investing in injection-mold tooling for ABS.

Designing without the hardware

I never had the circuit board in hand. I worked from photos instead, using the battery as a reference of known size to scale the board and derive every dimension.

Nine-plus iterations

Most iterations tightened the internal fit to remove play. The hardest detail was the lid. Its rim sits inside the body, so it had to be thin enough to sit flush yet thick enough to hold its position without deflecting. That landed at 0.5 mm.

The client accepted the final design and paid for the work.

Small business · 2021 – 2023

Custom Assyrian Name Prints

SoldAbout 100 custom pieces
CustomersMostly repeat
ProcessFDM, sliced in Cura
ScriptAssyrian

A product that didn't exist

Search for "Michael" desk art and you'll find endless options in English. In Assyrian, there was nothing like it. So I started making custom 3D-printed names in our script, and sold about 100 pieces, mostly to repeat customers.

Design problems the script created

  • Standing flat. Some letters drop below the writing line, and some consonants carry a dot underneath. Either one keeps a piece from sitting level, so I designed balance feet that carry the name at the right height.
  • One solid piece. Assyrian is a connected script, but a few letters never join the next one. After converting each name to an extrusion, I bridged those gaps by hand so every name prints as a single part.

Smaller projects and experiments

2026 · PLA + TPU · press fit

Guitar Hanger

A wall-mounted hanger with a rigid PLA frame and press-fit TPU inserts that protect the guitar's finish.

I sized the press fit using worst-case tolerances, assuming both the TPU and the PLA print larger than modeled, and dialed it in over three test prints. Then I compared infill patterns and chose 5% 3D honeycomb, which compresses evenly under the downward load of the headstock.

Experiment · mass analysis

Water Bottle Lab

Newer bottles from one brand were going limp. My hypothesis: the maker had thinned the walls to save plastic. I took apart a firm bottle (A) and a limp one (B), weighed every component, and ran the same test on a rigid and a brittle corn-based cup.

MeasureAB
Bottle body7 g11 g
Water held512 mL441 mL
Plastic per mL0.0137 g0.0249 g
Cup10.08 g7.41 g
RejectedThe limp bottle used about 58% more plastic per mL, not less.
SupportedThe brittle cup was about 30% lighter, consistent with a thinner wall.

The bottles' failure points to something else: a different blow-molding design, uneven wall distribution, a different resin, or a defect. I also flagged my own error source. Body mass was only measured to the nearest gram.

First class project · surgical tubing · CNC-cut PVC

Ball Launcher

A ball cannon powered by surgical tubing, built to hit a target within a set range. It was my first class project after getting into 3D printing.

The further you pull the ball holder back, the more power it has. A trigger locks the holder into a slot at that setting until you trip it. The barrel's channels were CNC-cut from PVC pipe. Aimed by sight, it had a range of about 30 feet.

Society of Manufacturing Engineers · May 2026

Bed leveling I wrote by hand

Before I had auto-leveling, I taught myself G-code and wrote a calibration routine that my slicer ran before every print. The nozzle traces the bed's perimeter while extruding, so I could turn each leveling knob and watch the line change in real time. Then it repeats in a smaller square, about five times, until the bed is level.

Circles mark the leveling knobs. The nozzle moves along one axis at a time, extruding, while I adjust.

About

From quarantine CAD to biomaterials

I learned 3D modeling during quarantine because I was bored. With money from a new job at Jamba Juice, I bought a computer and started designing out of curiosity. Soon I wanted my models to exist in real life, so I bought a 3D printer. I haven't stopped since.

Now I'm studying Materials Engineering at San José State, focused on biomedical materials. I'm especially interested in biocompatible polymers, and in tissue and organ printing, where materials engineering and additive manufacturing meet.

Before SJSU, I spent three years as a peer tutor at Modesto Junior College, covering math through Calculus IV, physics, chemistry, properties of materials, and engineering graphics.
